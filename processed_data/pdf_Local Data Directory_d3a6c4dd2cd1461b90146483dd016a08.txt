 A B S T R A C T
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable factual accuracy in producing 
human-like and AI-generated texts across a wide range of natural language tasks, includ-
ing question answering. Despite these advances, their tendency to hallucinate and produce 
fabricated, false or incorrect responses is a persistent limitation. This limitation undermines 
their reliability and remains a critical challenge, especially in areas where high precision and 
trustworthiness are required. To address this challenge, we investigate whether the features 
derived from Knowledge Graphs (KGs) align with the accuracy of answers produced by the 
LLMs. In particular, we focus on entropy-based KG features, which capture diversity and 
uncertainty within structured knowledge. By analyzing the correlation between the entropy-
based KG features and the accuracy of LLM responses, we are able to identify ‘‘blind spots’’ 
where LLMs are prone to hallucination. This provides insights not only into when an LLM is 
correct, but also into the conditions under which it fails. We present results across several 
datasets, including two developed for this study, demonstrating that entropy-based KG features 
can effectively align with the accuracy of LLM responses. Motivated by these findings, we 
propose a probing strategy for assessing LLM accuracy by focusing on areas where LLM accuracy 
is weak. The experimental results confirm that KG features can guide the probing effectively, 
highlighting the importance of using structured features from KGs in building more reliable and 
hallucination-free AI based systems.
1. Introduction
In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have exhibited significant advancements, achieving state-of-the-art performance 
across a wide range of natural language processing (NLP) tasks, including technical writing, question answering, code generation, and 
even multi-modal applications such as image generation. Despite these significant capabilities, LLMs exhibit a persistent limitation:
the generation of hallucinated content, i.e., information that is factually incorrect or unverifiable through external knowledge sources. 
Such tendencies undermine their reliability, particularly in knowledge-intensive domains and decision-making applications where 
factual consistency is critical.
To address this issue, recent research has explored the coupling of Knowledge Graphs (KGs) with LLMs to improve their factual 
accuracy by providing contextual grounding. The Knowledge injection approach integrates structured facts or entity embeddings 
into model parameters during pretraining (e.g., KnowBERT [1]), enhancing factual recall but requiring model retraining. Retrieval

Alignment and evaluation frameworks—including retrofitting methods [2], KGLens [3], and GraphEval [4]—probe LLM outputs 
against KG facts to identify ‘‘blind spots’’ or hallucinations. These methods improve interpretability, but require repeated fact-level 
verification, which can be computationally costly.
Motivated by the need for computational efficiency, we introduce an entropy driven KG-derived features-based approach to 
predict unaligned answers generated by LLMs. Our method employs a probing strategy guided by features derived from knowledge 
graphs, with a particular focus on structural aspects such as connectivity and specificity. We hypothesize that the structure of 
a graph can indicate how effectively an LLM is able to answer questions. Unlike LLMs, which are typically trained on text 
corpora from sources such as Wikipedia,1 knowledge graphs such as Wikidata [5] and DBpedia [6] provide structured and graph-
based information. Knowledge graphs represent data in the form of triples: (subject, predicate, and object). In resources such as 
Wikidata and DBpedia, entities represent real-world items (e.g., in Wikidata, Q42 denotes the author Douglas Adams; in DBpedia,
dbr:Barack_Obama denotes the U.S. president Barack Obama) and are connected to other entities through relations (e.g., P19
in Wikidata and dbo:birthPlace in DBpedia, both indicating place of birth).
We hypothesize that KG-derived features, such as the notion of entropy, can be used as a measure of uncertainty or diversity in 
the distribution of linked KG elements, such as property values or outgoing relations. Alongside entropy, we also examine structural 
features, including the number of statements and number of references, which may reflect the informativeness or coverage of an 
entity. To evaluate this relationship, we compute the Pearson correlation coefficient, which measures the strength and direction of 
the linear association between KG-derived features (e.g., property entropy, number of references) and correctness of answers. Our 
experiments use four datasets drawn from two distinct KGs: Wikidata and DBpedia. Each dataset contains natural language questions 
paired with corresponding SPARQL queries to obtain the actual answers from the respective KGs. Both Wikidata and DBpedia cover 
a broad range of topics and domains, enabling us to explore how KG features influence the performance of various open-source 
LLMs across different areas of knowledge. We hypothesize that these features are associated with greater variability in the data, 
making it harder for LLMs to answer.
An overview of the proposed framework is shown in Fig.  1. The framework first quantifies the relationship between language 
model accuracy and structured knowledge complexity through correlation analysis of entropy-based features derived from Wikidata 
and DBpedia. These correlations reveal which aspects of graph structure most strongly influence model behavior. Building on these 
findings, a probing strategy is then applied to evaluate model sensitivity and hallucination risk by selectively varying graph features, 
allowing us to identify cases where model outputs deviate from factual consistency despite structural predictability. The main 
contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
(1) Generation of a challenging Wikidata-based questions answers (Q/As) dataset for evaluating LLMs. In addition, we present 
DBpedia queries derived from Wikidata SPARQL queries.
(2) A systematic correlation analysis between entropy-based and structural KG features and LLM Q/As performance, showing 
that these features provide measurable signals predictive of hallucination likelihood when evaluated on four benchmarks 
generated over two different KGs, marking the generalizability of our approach to be adopted for other KGs.
(3) An entropy-guided probing strategy that identifies high-risk questions using KG features, thereby reducing the need for 
exhaustive alignment or querying.
(4) A comparison of our probing strategy with both a random probing and a simplified variant of KGLens [3], demonstrating 
that entropy-based selection effectively highlights factual inconsistencies.
(5) A systematic analysis of how knowledge graph–derived signals can be leveraged to assess and improve LLM behavior, 
suggesting pathways for integrating structural knowledge into model alignment and training.

2. Related work
Large Language Models (LLMs) struggle with producing unverifiable or unfaithful responses [8], a phenomenon commonly 
referred to as hallucination [9]. Studies on this problem have identified several distinct failure modes that motivated different lines 
of mitigation work. One well-studied problem is factuality hallucination, where a model generates content that contradicts verifiable 
real-world facts [9]. Another, complementary problem is faithfulness hallucination, where the model’s output diverges from the user-
provided context or becomes internally inconsistent [9]. Beyond these, LLMs also exhibit a tendency towards factual fabrication,

real-world source [9]. This tendency to furnish incorrect or fabricated details about real-world subjects poses a significant challenge, 
as even advanced models may generate references that are inaccurate or completely unfounded. To assess these behaviors, Lin et al. 
introduced TruthfulQA, a benchmark designed to measure whether models produce truthful answers to questions that commonly 
elicit false beliefs [10], while Min et al. proposed FActScore, which decomposes model outputs into atomic facts and verifies each 
against a knowledge source [11].
Various methods have been proposed to assess and mitigate their tendency of producing hallucinated content. A common way is 
fine-tuning, which further improved to be parameter efficient fine-tuning technique [12]. However, this method requires extensive 
computational resources to be implemented for each model. This problem, shifted the paradigm towards internal verification or self-
evaluation [13,14] where LLMs responses are assessed by themselves. Due to constant updates and exposure to unseen scenarios, 
models suffer from the self-blindness problem, which causes factual errors in their responses. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) 
has been proposed to verify model response against external documents [15–17]. Yet, retrieved passages are often lack correctness 
or completeness, resulting in residual hallucinations [18]. To improve grounding, structured resources like Knowledge Graphs (KGs) 
provide a reliable and interpretable means for verification.
A growing body of research has investigated the alignment of LLM outputs with KGs [19], showing that the integration of 
structured resources is closely associated with improvements in factual accuracy and reasoning capabilities [20–22] of LLMs. Zhou 
et al. [23] address the dual challenges of LLM hallucination and KG incompleteness, but continuously expanding and aligning a KG 
in real time poses scalability and incurs high computational costs due to supervised fine-tuning as, it requires labeled data. Guan 
et al. [2] tried to mitigate model hallucination by grounding responses in Wikidata using few-shot prompting, but due to the poor 
fact selection and limited reasoning ability of the model, it could not fully address the problem. The work most closely related to 
our method is KGLens [3], which generates questions from sampled triples and repeatedly probes the model to identify regions in 
KGs prone to hallucinations. Although effective, this method becomes computationally expensive for large graphs, as it requires 
generating and evaluating queries for each triple in the KG to assess the LLM’s responses. In contrast, our approach relies on a 
limited set of queries to sample property and entity entropy, together with metadata, via the Wikidata API.
Other strategies include the generation of structured KGs from LLM outputs and the evaluation of the accuracy of generated 
graph triples with actual KGs [24], While these methods risk information loss from parsing limitations, Zhu et al. [25] propose 
a reverse strategy that generates false-premise questions from KGs to uncover factual inconsistencies. Several recent studies have 
also explored the adapters from KGs to improve the LLMs performance [26,27]; however, these approaches also require substantial 
computational resources to handle millions of entities in KGs. Some other methods uncover the potential of aligning KGs with LLMs 
to stimulate the reasoning capabilities of LLMs [28–30]. We do not consider aligning KGs and LLMs to check the reasoning of LLMs 
over given KG paths, although there is potential to explore this in future work.

answers for the evaluation. To support this process, we extracted the proposed features from two general-purpose KGs that cover 
various domains. KGs details that are used in this study are given below.

3.1. Knowledge graphs utilized
We choose Wikidata and DBpedia to extract the proposed structural features, as these graphs complement each other in both 
structure and coverage: where Wikidata has up-to-date and fresh facts, while DBpedia has a stable ontology.
• Wikidata is one of the most widely used multilingual knowledge bases. Its schema is community driven, allowing dynamic 
property creation. The Wikidata KG is continuously updated, with explicitly encoded references for most claims. Moreover, It 
serves as a structured repository for both Wikidata and Wikimedia projects. Additionally, as of October 2025, it contains up 
to 119 million items, with more than 2.4 billion edits since its launch.
• DBpedia contains approximately 9.5 billion resource description framework (RDF) triples generated from Wikipedia projects. 
It follows a static ontology and relies on Wikipedia info-boxes, resulting in greater stability but less exposure to new knowledge. 
Also, it does not record references like Wikidata.
Therefore, both KGs cover a wide range of general knowledge-related entities, which aligns with the training data of most 
general-purpose LLMs that we will use in our study.

4. Datasets
This section explains the dataset details used in this study. As we mentioned in Section 3, testing our hypothesis requires natural 
language questions, LLM answers, and equivalent KG queries. To meet these requirements, we use four benchmark datasets that 
satisfy the necessary criteria and cover two different KGs. Each dataset covers one KG (as shown in Table  2). The following are the 
details of the benchmarks we used in this paper.
4.1. WikiWebQuestions Q/As dataset
In this work, we employ the WikiWebQuestions [31] dataset, which serves as a question answering (QA) benchmark for 
evaluating QA systems that rely on structured knowledge graphs like Wikidata. It bridges natural language questions with structured 
data through SPARQL queries to retrieve answers from Wikidata. It includes 3665 training and 2032 test questions, covering diverse 
entities and relations. We adapt it for a different purpose than the authors (because it satisfies our requirements even though it was 
developed for a different purpose), to measure LLM answer accuracy by aligning it with Wikidata’s structural features. To assess 
the correlation between proposed KG features and LLM answer for each question type, we split this and the subsequent datasets 
except WikidataComplexQuestions dataset, into single-answer and multiple-answer questions. Moreover, our method requires Q/As 
set with corresponding SPARQL query containing at-least one entity (represented as node) and one property (predicate). Therefore, 
we removed all entries that did not satisfy these constraints resulting in 941 single-answer and 835 multiple-answers questions. 
The dataset is publicly available on Kaggle.2 We also exploit WikiWebQuestions to generate data from DBpedia as described in Sub 
Section 4.4.
4.2. LC-QuAD complex Q/A dataset
LC-QuAD [32] dataset was originally created to generate complex Q/As over KGs using a mixed approach–automatic generation 
of questions followed by manual correction to ensure its quality. It comes with 5000 natural language questions paired with SPARQL 
queries over DBpedia. The authors built this dataset using DBpedia version 04-2016, but we fetch the actual answers for the given

unable to compute the proposed features for some of the entities and properties. An example of a query generation prompt is shown

5. LLMs answer generation and accuracy evaluation
To analyze the correlation between KG features and LLM accuracy, we generate responses from LLMs and evaluate their 
correctness. To aid in reproducibility, we used the live Wikidata and DBpedia endpoints. For this study, we mainly used open-source 
LLMs, as they are freely accessible and cost-effective. We used the closed-source GPT-4o mini model only for the LC-QuAD dataset to 
evaluate its performance, as other open-source LLMs exhibit weak factual accuracy due to the challenging questions in the dataset.

5.2. LLM answer evaluation
To evaluate the correctness of LLM answers, we adopt a classification approach based on the correctness and relevance of 
generated responses using GPT-4o9 by providing the actual answer, LLM answer and asking GPT-4o to classify the model answer 
into one of the following 7 categories (see A.3 for an example of the prompt):
(1) Perfectly accurate: The answer is fully correct, clear, and directly addresses the question.
(2) Mostly accurate: The answer is largely correct, with only minor wording or nuance differences, and no real errors.
(3) Accurate with irrelevance: The answer is correct but includes extra or unnecessary details.
(4) Inaccurate (Intrinsic error, e.g., outdated or erroneous training data): The answer is wrong, probably due to limitations 
in the model’s training data.
(5) Inaccurate (fabricated/hallucinated): The answer is wrong and seems made up or unsupported from external facts.
(6) Inaccurate (off-topic): The answer is wrong because it addresses a different question or goes off-topic.
(7) Completely inaccurate: The answer is entirely wrong or is not related to the question.
We manually inspected a representative sample of the generated responses to verify the reliability and consistency of the LLM-
based categorizations, consistent with prior work that shows that recent LLM evaluators can achieve high agreement with human 
judgments [33]. By categorizing LLM responses, we analyze each model’s performance across different benchmarks and the impact 
of structural entropy-based KG features, where higher entropy may correlate with greater uncertainty or hallucination. While Models 
may perform better on lower-entropy features. With these correlation results, we gain a clearer understanding of how structured 
knowledge representations influence LLM response accuracy. Also, our analysis shows that these features can serve as predictors of 
hallucination.
6. Correlation analysis between knowledge graph features and LLM correctness
The proposed method is tested against four datasets built over two general-domain KGs (Wikidata and DBpedia). We treat 
categories 1–3 as factually correct and 4–7 as factually incorrect responses. To assess the robustness of proposed KG-based 
features, we compute the Pearson correlation between each feature and model performance, followed by the examination of their 
corresponding p-values to assess the statistical significance. A 𝑝-value less than 0.05 indicates that the correlation is statistically 
significant at the 95% confidence level. The correlation results for each benchmark are shown in the following subsections:
6.1. WikiWebQuestions
The LLM answer distribution for the WikiWebQuestions dataset is shown in Fig.  2. The Pearson correlation of each feature with 
LLM accuracy (1–3: correct, 4–7: incorrect) is shown in Fig.  3, where * on each bar indicates a significant Pearson correlation 
(p_value < 0.05). For the WikiWebQuestions dataset, all models (0-6) show statistically significant correlation with all the proposed 
features except SPARQL complexity, which shows no significance for single answer questions and only partial significance (in 3 out 
6 models) for multi-answer questions. This indicates that these features reliably align with models behavior on this benchmarks, 
confirming their utility for probing KG-LLM alignment. As expected, both entity entropy and property entropy correlate 
negatively with model accuracy, which means that entities and properties with higher entropy represent greater uncertainty or 
diversity in the distribution of linked values, resulting in hallucination or falsehood in the model response. In contrast, the last 
modified date exhibits a positive correlation with model accuracy, suggesting that recently updated entities are more likely to 
be present in the training data, enhancing factual recall. Similarly, the number of statements, properties, references, and sitelinks 
demonstrates positive correlations, indicating that entities with richer structural information and higher connectivity are better 
grounded in the training data, enabling models to provide more accurate answers.
6.2. LC-QuAD dataset
Based on the WikiWebQuestions results we extended our experiments for LC-QuAD dataset which is constructed on a different 
KG (DBpedia), to see whether feature-performance relationship generalize to other KGs. The Pearson correlation between proposed 
features and model responses for LC-QuAD dataset is shown in Fig.  5. Unlike WikiWebQuestions, most open-source models showed 
weaker or statistically insignificant correlations for several features, except for entity entropy and number of statements. Six of seven 
models for single answer and five for multiple answers maintained a significant positive correlation with entity entropy.

7. Probing strategy and evaluation
In the previous section, we showed how our approach can be effective in KG-LLM alignment. Here, we apply this method to 
select question–answer pairs that are more likely to be misaligned with the KG and evaluate them against the Mistral-7B model. We

compare this probing strategy against two baselines: (i) random probing, where 𝑁 Q/As are randomly selected from the dataset, 
and (ii) a parameterized subgraph-based approximation approach based on KGLens [3].
The original KGLens approach probes subsets of the Wikidata KG to assess their alignment with LLM responses, whereas our 
WikiWebQuestions-based setting includes arbitrary queries spanning the entire Wikidata. As of December 2025, Wikidata contains 
1.65 billion statements, making it infeasible to cover the entire dataset. Therefore, we adopt a simplified approximation of KGLens 
described here. Starting from a seed entity, we extract its direct neighbors and then expand once more from each first-hop neighbor 
to form a two-hop neighborhood, limiting the expansion to at most 50 edges per hop to retain tractability. We filter all the triples to 
retain only triples with meaningful factual relations and human-readable English labels, i.e., we discard links to external databases, 
etc., resulting in a two-hop subgraph of the Wikidata-KG centered on the seed entity. Each triple (𝑠, 𝑝, 𝑜) in the subgraph is associated 
with a confidence value representing the model’s likelihood of failing on that fact. Following [3], this confidence is modeled as a 
Beta-distributed random variable where 𝜃𝑠,𝑝,𝑜 ∼ Beta( 𝛼𝑠,𝑝,𝑜, 𝛽𝑠,𝑝,𝑜) denotes the probability of the LLM failing to answer the question 
about that fact. All triples start with a uniform prior: 𝛼 = 1, 𝛽 = 1. After querying the model, these parameters are updated based on 
its performance: if the model answers incorrectly, 𝛼 ← 𝛼 + 1; if correctly, 𝛽 ← 𝛽 + 1. These updates are propagated to neighboring 
edges as well, i.e., all triples sharing the same subject or object—under the assumption that related facts share correlated knowledge 
gaps. At each iteration, we select the triple with the highest expected failure probability 𝜃𝑠,𝑝,𝑜 for querying. For simplicity, and with 
a slight deviation from the method in [3], at each iteration, we select the triple with the highest expected failure probability 𝜃𝑠,𝑝,𝑜
and generate questions for each selected triple with the template as follows: ‘‘What is the relation between s and o?’’
where 𝑠 and 𝑜 are replaced with the subject and object entities in the triple. The generated question is prompted to the LLM and 
the answer is evaluated against the actual 𝑝 value from Wikidata-KG. We use another model (GPT4o-mini) to evaluate the model 
response. The evaluation prompt contains the question, the model’s answer, and the actual Wikidata predicate and query: ‘‘Does 
the answer match this information? The correct relation is: <relation>’’.

8. From correlation to application: Practical utilization of entropy-guided insights
This section outlines how the proposed entropy-guided approach can inform the improvement of large language models, offering 
insights for developers and researchers seeking to enhance factual grounding, reduce hallucination, and train language models more 
efficiently.
(1) Training data: It sample more training data from entities and properties with high entropy, since these correspond to regions 
where LLMs are most uncertain or hallucinate, thereby helps the model to handle uncertainty and improve its factual accuracy 
effectively. It prioritize the inclusion of low-frequency or sparsely linked entities, ensuring diverse structural coverage in the 
dataset. Also, it refreshes training data for entities whose entropy or last-modified timestamps changes rapidly ensuring factual

(2) Evaluation: Evaluates LLMs knowledge in certain domains more effectively by using high entropy samples reflecting real 
world challenges. Identify systematic failure clusters by analyzing questions associated with high-entropy features. Use 
entropy–accuracy correlation coefficients as an additional diagnostic metric to compute confidence or model answer accuracy.

9. Conclusions and future work
In this paper, we proposed that correlations may exist between LLM answer accuracy and KG structural features. We tested this 
hypothesis by extracting KG features and analyzing their correlations across four datasets targeting two general-purpose knowledge 
graphs. Our findings demonstrate that KG structural features correlate with LLM answer quality, particularly in the case of Wikidata, 
whose rich relational structure and curated references amplify the impact of entropy-based features on model accuracy. In particular, 
entities associated with higher structural uncertainty and weaker supporting graph structure were more likely to correspond to 
lower-quality or less reliable generated answers, while richer and more densely connected graph regions were generally associated 
with more stable responses.
Moreover, our entropy-based probing method highlights blind spots where KGs and LLMs are not aligned, supporting more 
efficient LLM evaluation and providing insight into when external structured knowledge may be beneficial. These findings also 
suggest several practical mitigation strategies, including uncertainty-aware gating, selective KG injection, deeper retrieval, and 
additional verification steps for high-risk entities or graph regions. Rather than uniformly applying KG augmentation, our results 
indicate that KG structural signals may help determine when external knowledge is likely to improve answer quality and when it 
may instead introduce additional noise.
In future work, we will further investigate these LLM-engineering implications and explore practical applications of adaptive 
KG-aware generation strategies, as outlined in Section 8.

Research Institute, National Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST), Japan. His research interests include database technologies and 
graph processing. He is a member of DBSJ, IEICE, and IPSJ.
Toshiyuki Amagasa received B.E., M.E., and Ph.D. degrees from the Department of Computer Science, Gunma University in 1994, 1996, and 1999, respectively. 
He is currently a full professor at the Center for Computational Sciences (CCS) and the Center for Artificial Intelligence Research (C-AIR), University of Tsukuba. 
His research interests cover data engineering, database systems, data mining, and database applications in scientific domains. He is a senior member of IPSJ, 
IEICE, and IEEE, a board member of DBSJ, and a member of ACM.
